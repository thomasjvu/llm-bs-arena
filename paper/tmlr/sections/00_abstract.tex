\begin{abstract}
Large language models increasingly operate in multi-step settings where strategic misrepresentation can improve local outcomes, yet most empirical work on honesty still centers on single-turn question answering or static preference probes. We introduce \textbf{Bullshit-Bench}, a reproducible benchmark harness for studying deception and instruction compliance by having four LLM agents play the card game \emph{Bullshit}. The environment is useful because lying is a legal action, the truth of each claim is objectively verifiable from hidden cards, and outcomes depend on both bluffing and lie detection under uncertainty. We evaluate six hosted models under four prompt conditions: a low-strategy reference, a deception-allowed baseline, an asymmetric-fairness condition, and a plain-language honesty mandate. The system combines a seeded TypeScript engine, provider-backed execution, provenance-aware logging, long-run failure recovery, and a player-game analysis pipeline. The frozen comparable cohort contains 600 winner-terminated games with no mixed-cohort or turn-cap exclusions. The main empirical result is that explicit honesty instructions reduce deceptive play but do not eliminate it: mean lie frequency falls from 38.5\% in the deception-allowed baseline to 26.5\% under the honesty mandate, while mean challenge frequency falls from 40.6\% to 20.0\% and mean lie success rises from 12.4\% to 36.2\%. The asymmetric-fairness condition yields heterogeneous model-level lie-frequency shifts ranging from -7.9 to +0.4 percentage points rather than a universal restraint effect. These results show that prompt framing changes the equilibrium of the table, not just the liar's behavior.
\end{abstract}
