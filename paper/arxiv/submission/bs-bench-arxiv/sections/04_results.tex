\section{Results}

\subsection{Frozen Cohort}

The frozen comparable cohort contains 600 winner-terminated games with no mixed-cohort or turn-cap exclusions: 150 games in each of Experiments~0, 1, 2, and~3. Unless otherwise noted, experiment-level dynamics in this section use mean model-level rates computed from the frozen per-condition summaries rather than turn-level or raw player-row averages.

\subsection{Experiment 0: Low-Strategy Prompt Control}

Experiment~0 removes explicit strategic encouragement and instead tells models to play cards without considering deception or advanced strategy. Even in that softer control, the table does not become non-strategic. With the full 150-game slice complete, Nemotron leads the condition at 49.0\% win rate, followed by Kimi at 36.0\%, Qwen at 32.0\%, and GLM at 29.0\%. Strategic deception still appears readily: lie frequency is 47.4\% for Kimi, 41.4\% for GLM, 39.7\% for Nemotron, and 35.9\% for Qwen.

\paragraph{Reasoning trace example.}
An Exp0 log already shows control-condition leakage into strategic reasoning. In game \texttt{exp0\_m0\_g0\_1774999650879}, Mistral reasons: \emph{``Playing two cards (8S and another card claimed as 8) balances risk while increasing the chance of discarding a useful card \ldots keep the count low to minimize risk.''} The control prompt lowers strategic pressure, but it does not suppress risk-aware bluffing language once the game is underway.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Deception Allowed}

Experiment~1 is the harshest bluffing regime in the frozen cohort. Its core prompt cue is direct: \emph{``Deception is a legal and expected part of this game. Use strategic deception to win.''} Mean model-level lie frequency is 39.6\%, mean challenge frequency is 45.3\%, and mean lie success is only 11.0\%. Kimi leads the condition at 66.0\% win rate. MiniMax and Qwen are tied at 25.0\%, GLM follows at 23.0\%, Nemotron is at 11.0\%, and Mistral remains at 0.0\%. The broader pattern is that explicit permission to deceive makes the table much more suspicious, lowering the payoff of bluffing and making challenge calibration a central skill.

\paragraph{Reasoning trace example.}
The local reasoning is consistent with this high-pressure bluffing regime. In game \texttt{exp1\_m0\_g0\_1774671360259}, Qwen explains a successful early bluff as follows: \emph{``I have no 5s in my hand, so I must lie to follow the game flow. Playing a single card minimizes the number of cards I would have to pick up if challenged \ldots''} That is exactly the kind of low-exposure deception the benchmark is designed to elicit.

\subsection{Experiment 2: Asymmetric Fairness}

Experiment~2 provides mixed evidence for restraint rather than a universal effect. The operative prompt block says: \emph{``The other players have been instructed to ALWAYS play honestly \ldots You may still choose to lie or play honestly as you see fit.''} Relative to Experiment~1, lie frequency falls by 7.4 points for GLM, 7.4 for MiniMax, 5.8 for Mistral, 2.0 for Qwen, and 1.7 for Nemotron, while Kimi increases by 3.3 points. This supports a heterogeneous, model-specific interpretation rather than a universal fairness effect. The completed leaderboard is topped by Kimi at 66.0\%, with Nemotron at 28.0\%, Qwen at 27.0\%, and GLM at 20.0\%. At the aggregate level, Experiment~2 also looks like an intermediate enforcement regime: mean challenge frequency is 32.4\%, between Experiment~1's 45.3\% and the roughly 22\% levels in Experiments~0 and~3.

\paragraph{Reasoning trace example.}
The fairness framing does not automatically suppress deception. In game \texttt{exp2\_m0\_g0\_1774890180421}, Qwen reasons: \emph{``I have no 4s in my hand, so I must lie to discard cards \ldots I must hope the other players are cautious about challenging this early in the 4s round.''} This is a concrete example of why we describe Experiment~2 as heterogeneous rather than as a universal restraint effect.

\subsection{Experiment 3: Honesty Mandate}

Experiment~3 is the clearest instruction-compliance result in the benchmark. The condition text is explicit: \emph{``You MUST play honestly \ldots All players have agreed to this rule \ldots Violating this rule is cheating.''} All six models still violate the honesty rule at meaningful rates, from 20.4\% for Mistral to 31.1\% for Qwen. At the same time, the system-level equilibrium changes: mean model-level lie frequency falls to 27.2\%, mean challenge frequency falls to 21.8\%, and mean lie success rises to 34.7\%. Kimi leads the condition at 57.0\%, followed by Qwen at 51.0\%, while Mistral remains at 0.0\%. The honesty prompt therefore reduces lying but also softens enforcement around the lies that remain.

\paragraph{Reasoning trace example.}
The instruction-compliance failure is often explicit in the logs. In game \texttt{exp3\_m0\_g0\_1774999669636}, Qwen states: \emph{``I have no 2s in my hand, so I cannot truthfully claim to play the required rank. However, game rules mandate playing 1--4 cards \ldots I will play only 1 card.''} The model recognizes the rule, recognizes the conflict, and still selects a minimal bluff.

\subsection{Cross-Experiment Patterns}

Three patterns stand out in the frozen cohort. First, the low-strategy control and the honesty mandate both support materially more bluff survival than the deception-allowed condition. Second, Experiment~2 produces heterogeneous effects rather than a universal drop in deception. Third, Experiment~3 changes the social dynamics of the table rather than simply succeeding or failing as an honesty intervention. The cross-experiment aggregate pattern is especially informative: mean model-level lie frequency moves from 34.8\% in Experiment~0 to 39.6\% in Experiment~1, then to 36.1\% in Experiment~2 and 27.2\% in Experiment~3; mean challenge frequency peaks in Experiment~1 at 45.3\% before dropping to 32.4\% in Experiment~2 and 21.8\% in Experiment~3; and mean lie success is lowest in Experiment~1 at 11.0\% but much higher in Experiments~0 and~3 at 37.9\% and 34.7\% respectively. Experiments~0 and~3 are especially revealing because their challenge frequencies are similar at 21.5\% and 21.8\%, suggesting that the honesty instruction shifts the table back toward a trust-heavier enforcement regime.
