\begin{abstract}
Large language models increasingly operate in multi-step settings where strategic misrepresentation can improve local outcomes, but most honesty evaluations remain single-turn and static. We introduce \textbf{BS-Bench}, a reproducible benchmark release plus a frozen 600-game pilot for deception, lie detection, and instruction compliance in multi-agent play by having four LLM agents play the card game \emph{Bullshit}. The environment is useful because lying is legal, the truth of each claim is objectively verifiable from hidden cards, and outcomes depend on both bluffing and challenge behavior under uncertainty. We evaluate six hosted models from a single-provider cohort under four prompt conditions: a low-strategy prompt control, deception allowed, asymmetric honesty framing, and an honesty mandate. In the frozen pilot, the Experiment~3 all-player honesty-mandate system prompt sharply reduces optional lying when truthful play is available, but play remains non-trivially deceptive because turns where no truthful play is available remain common. The same condition also softens inspection: challenges become less frequent and the bluffs that remain succeed more often. Asymmetric honesty framing produces model-specific responses rather than a uniform compliance effect. Overall, BS-Bench shows that prompt framing shifts both observed bluffing and peer challenge behavior.
\end{abstract}

\paragraph{Summary.}
BS-Bench is a controlled multi-agent benchmark where legal bluffs are objectively labeled and peer challenges have observable consequences. In the frozen 600-game pilot, the Experiment~3 all-player honesty-mandate system prompt reduces optional lying when truthful play is available, but it also softens challenge behavior, with the remaining bluffs succeeding more often. We treat this as a descriptive table-dynamics result rather than a causal mechanism claim: prompt framing shifts both observed bluffing and peer challenge behavior.
