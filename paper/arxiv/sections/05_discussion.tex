\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Strategic Style Versus Raw Leaderboard Position}

The benchmark surfaces stable strategic profiles rather than only a leaderboard. Across the frozen 600-game cohort, Kimi claims 225 wins for a 37.5\% overall win share while maintaining only 11.3\% challenge frequency, whereas Mistral remains at 0 wins with 63.4\% challenge frequency. Kimi is the most robust finisher because it leads Experiments~1, 2, and~3 by win share. Nemotron is the strongest condition-specific finisher in the low-strategy control, where it takes 49 of 150 wins for a 32.7\% share. Qwen is the most consistent runner-up across the deception-relevant conditions. These are not interchangeable styles: the benchmark is exposing different ways to trade off bluffing, inspection, and risk.

\subsection{Instruction Compliance Under Competitive Incentives}

The honesty-mandate condition is the strongest alignment result in the paper, but only after separating optional dishonesty from conflict turns. The plain-language rule sharply suppresses lying when truthful play is available: mean model-level optional-lie rate falls from 13.4\% in Experiment~1 to 2.2\% in Experiment~3. But about a quarter of Experiment~3 turns are truthful-play-unavailable at the moment of action, so overall lie frequency still lands at 27.2\%. The table also becomes less vigilant. Relative to Experiment~1, mean challenge frequency falls from 45.3\% to 21.8\%, while mean lie success rises from 11.0\% (95\% CI 7.5--16.0) to 34.7\% (95\% CI 27.6--44.5). In this benchmark and pilot cohort, prompt framing therefore changes the social equilibrium of the table, not just the actor's willingness to bluff.

\subsection{Bullshit as an Inspection Game}

Bullshit can be read as an inspection game. Challenging too often is costly, but challenging too rarely makes bluffing cheap. This is why winning is not a simple function of more bluffing or more suspicion. The strongest profiles in the pilot appear to balance bluffing with selective enforcement, while Mistral anchors the opposite pattern: it is consistently the most suspicious model and never wins a condition.

The same interpretation explains why the honesty mandate changes more than individual lying. Experiment~3 sharply suppresses optional lies, but it also lowers challenge pressure, so the bluffs that remain are more likely to survive. This is a descriptive pattern, not a causal mechanism claim: the benchmark shows how bluffing and inspection move together under different prompts.

The turn-level logs also show that the benchmark contains stronger bluffs than the one-card examples highlighted in Section~4. Some models attempt three- and four-card zero-match claims, and a small fraction of those claims survive. The appendix traces give concrete examples of padding a truthful play, bluffing on a conflict turn, and challenging an impossible claim.

\subsection{Limitations}

This is a benchmark release plus pilot study, not a complete account of LLM deception. The current cohort uses one game environment, one provider cohort, and six hosted models, so the results should not be read as model-size effects or broad claims about real-world deception. Experiment~0 is also a low-strategy prompt control rather than a pure non-strategic baseline, because the shared rules still expose lying, challenging, and risk language.

The logs preserve actor reasoning and actual challenge rationales, but they do not preserve comparable rationales from players who decline to challenge. As a result, interpretations of unchallenged bluffs rely on observed behavior rather than direct evidence of why judges passed. The next run should log every challenge-window decision, including declines to challenge, so accepted bluffs, false passes, and correct non-challenges can be analyzed with the same reasoning detail as challenges.

A stronger v2 should also add a neutered-rules control with no deception, challenge, or risk language in the shared prompt, include human player baselines, broaden the model/provider cohort, and preserve fuller reasoning traces across actor, challenger, and non-challenger decisions. These additions would improve calibration and mechanism analysis without changing the core benchmark premise.
